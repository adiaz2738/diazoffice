If you've ever tried to find a house in Carmel-by-the-Sea, you know the town does things its own way. Until this fall, homes here didn't have street addresses. Locals gave directions like "Santa Rita, 3 southwest of 4th." There are no streetlights in the residential neighborhoods, no sidewalks outside downtown, and the roads bend around trees instead of the other way around.

None of that is an accident. Most of what makes Carmel feel different today goes back to a handful of decisions the town made in its first few decades.

## Before the village

Long before Carmel was a town, this was Rumsen Ohlone land. In 1771, Father Junípero Serra moved Mission San Carlos Borromeo from Monterey to the mouth of the Carmel River, where it still stands just outside today's city limits.

The first attempt at a town came in 1888, when Santiago Duckworth and Honoré Escolle filed a subdivision map. Duckworth hoped to build a Catholic summer resort, and he started selling lots. It never became the resort he had in mind, and much of the land was still unsold when the next developers came along with a different idea.

## A town built for artists

In 1902, Frank Devendorf and Frank Powers formed the Carmel Development Company and bought up the unsold land. Devendorf was a businessman with a love of land development, and Powers was an attorney. Devendorf would spend the next 30 years building Carmel into a community of painters, writers, and musicians.

They made buying in easy. Lots sold for around $500, secured with a $5 or $10 deposit. Devendorf loved trees and had a row of pines planted down the middle of Ocean Avenue, the town's main street. Builder Michael J. Murphy put up a large share of the early homes, nearly 350 buildings between 1902 and 1940.

Then came the 1906 San Francisco earthquake. Artists, writers, and musicians left the damaged city, and many of them landed in Carmel. Poet George Sterling and writer Mary Austin were among the early names that gave the village its reputation. Austin is credited with the idea for the Forest Theater, an outdoor stage that opened in 1910 and is still in use today.

## Keeping Carmel off the map

Carmel incorporated as a city in 1916, and its early leaders were protective of what they had. The village decided against sidewalks in the residential areas, against streetlights, and against commercial development on the beach, and it set out to protect the native trees.

In the 1920s, the city went further. It formally declared itself "primarily, essentially and predominantly" a residential community, with business taking a back seat. That language is still in the city's zoning code today. Around the same time, Perry Newberry, one of the town's leaders, campaigned on the slogan "Keep Carmel Off the Map."

The lack of street addresses came out of that same attitude. Instead of home mail delivery, which some residents felt would make the village feel too much like a city, everyone picked up their mail at the downtown post office. Many homes were known by name instead of number, and the trip to the post office became part of daily life.

## The fairy tale cottages

The storybook cottages that show up on so many postcards started almost by accident. In 1924, Hugh Comstock was visiting Carmel when his wife, Mayotta, asked him to build a small place to display her handmade "Otsy-Totsy" dolls. He built a tiny cottage with a steep roof and named it "Hansel."

People loved it. Comstock went on to design and build many more cottages around town, with steep gables, half-timbering, and diamond-paned windows. About 21 of his cottages are still standing in the Carmel area. Comstock later served on the city's planning commission, so he helped shape the look of Carmel in more ways than one.

## A town that still makes its own rules

Carmel's quirks have kept coming. In 1963, the city passed an ordinance requiring a permit to wear high heels over 2 inches tall. The idea was to protect the city from lawsuits, since tree roots buckle the pavement. The permits are free at City Hall. In 1986, Clint Eastwood was elected mayor and served one term.

The newest change is the biggest in a long time. In June 2026, the City Council unanimously approved a street address map, and numbers started going up on homes this fall. The main reason was practical. Ambulances had trouble finding homes, and more residents now rely on home deliveries for things like prescriptions. As Mayor Dale Byrne put it, "This is 2026, not 1906."

## What it means if you're buying or selling in Carmel

The same history that gives Carmel its charm also shapes how homes here are bought, sold, and changed:

- **Design and historic review.** Carmel reviews changes to homes closely, and many older homes are on the city's historic inventory. That can affect what you're able to remodel or rebuild.
- **Trees.** The town's early commitment to its forest is still in the rules. Removing or significantly trimming many trees requires city approval.
- **Small lots and older homes.** Many homes sit on small original lots, close to their neighbors. That's part of the village feel, and it affects things like parking, additions, and outdoor space.
- **Short-term rentals.** Short-term rentals aren't allowed in Carmel's single-family zone.

If you're looking at a home in Carmel, it's worth knowing these rules before you fall in love with a remodel plan. The [Buyer's Guide chapter on local rules](/buyers-guide/water-permits-local-rules) covers them in more detail.
