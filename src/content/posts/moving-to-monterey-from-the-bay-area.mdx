A lot of the buyers I meet are coming from out of the area: Sacramento, Texas, Arizona, and most of all, the Bay Area. Some are moving here full time, some are working remotely, and some are planning ahead for retirement. The draw is easy to understand: the coast, a slower pace, and, in many cases, more home for the money.

Moving here is a bigger change than the drive suggests, though. Here's what's different, and what to think about before you buy.

## What's different about the market here

**It's smaller.** Monterey County has far fewer homes for sale than the Bay Area, and not many for the number of people who live here. As of October 13, 2026, there were 619 homes for sale in the county on the MLS, for about 438,000 residents. That's roughly one home for sale for every 700 people. Under $1 million, there were just 219, or about one for every 2,000 people.

Inventory has actually grown over the last few years. In 2025 and 2026, the number of homes for sale has run well above where it was in 2021 and 2022. Even so, for a county this size, it's still a tight market.

Prices change over time, and medians don't tell you what any one home is worth. The mix of homes that sell in a given period also moves the median. A few more high-end sales, or a few more small homes and fixers, can push it up or down, especially in smaller markets with fewer sales.*

The gaps are big, even between neighbors. A lot of buyers who can't find what they want in Monterey end up looking in Seaside or Marina, and buyers priced out of Salinas often look south to Soledad, Gonzales, and Greenfield, trading a longer commute for a lower price. Within each town, values can change from one street to the next, as covered in the [Seller's Guide chapter on pricing](/sellers-guide/pricing-your-home).

**Seaside or Marina?** I get asked a lot which one is cheaper. For a long time, the answer was Marina. But so many of Marina's sales now are newer homes, and those cost more, that the gap has closed. As I write this, Marina's median sold price is actually higher than Seaside's. You can still sometimes find an older home in Marina for a little less, but the difference isn't big. In my opinion, if you don't need a brand-new home, Seaside is often the better value: many neighborhoods are closer to the ocean, and it tends to be less foggy than Marina.

**Each market moves at its own speed.** As I write this, Seaside and Salinas are some of the faster-moving markets in the county, with well-priced homes selling quickly. Monterey and Pacific Grove are more balanced. Carmel Valley is the slowest-moving market in the county right now. How fast a market moves affects how much room you have to negotiate, so it's worth asking about the specific area you're looking in.

**Homes are often older.** The median home in Monterey County is about 51 years old, and many neighborhoods on the Peninsula are much older than that. That's part of the charm, but it also means more attention to things like roofs, sewer lines, foundations, and older electrical systems.

**Local rules are stricter than you might expect.** Water limits on the Peninsula can make adding a bathroom difficult. Some cities require their own inspection reports before a sale. Historic review, tree permits, and short-term rental restrictions are common. [Chapter 6 of the Buyer's Guide](/buyers-guide/water-permits-local-rules) covers all of this.

**Insurance takes planning.** Like the rest of California, homeowners insurance here can be harder to get or more expensive for some homes, especially in fire zones. See [Chapter 7 of the Buyer's Guide](/buyers-guide/insurance-fire-flood).

## Commuting to the Bay Area

If you'll still be commuting, location matters a lot.

- **Driving.** From the Peninsula, San Jose is roughly 70 miles away. Without traffic, it's a little over an hour. With traffic, it can easily take an hour and a half or more each way. Living in North County, Salinas, or along Highway 101 shortens the drive.
- **Local traffic.** Getting off the Peninsula takes longer than you'd think at busy times. Highway 68, for example, backs up in the morning from Salinas toward Monterey, and in the late afternoon going the other way.
- **Train.** Right now, the only direct train from Salinas to the Bay Area is Amtrak's Coast Starlight, a long-distance train that runs between Seattle and Los Angeles. It stops at the Salinas station once a day in each direction, with Bay Area stops in San Jose and the East Bay, so it's not set up for a daily commute. Caltrain's southern end is in Gilroy, about 35 miles north of Salinas. Plans are in the works to run more trains between Salinas and San Jose, with service possibly starting as early as 2028, but that timeline has slipped before.

Many Bay Area buyers who move here work remotely or go into the office a day or two a week. If you'll be commuting most days, try the drive at the actual time you'd be making it before you buy.

## Where Bay Area buyers tend to look

Every area has a different feel. A few patterns I see:

- **Carmel, Pebble Beach, and Pacific Grove,** for the coast, older homes, and a village feel.
- **Monterey,** for a mix of neighborhoods close to downtown, the waterfront, and amenities.
- **Newer developments.** A lot of Bay Area buyers I meet are drawn to the newer communities, especially Seaside Highlands in Seaside and The Dunes in Marina, along with Sea Haven in Marina and East Garrison. Newer homes with fewer surprises are a big part of the appeal.
- **Seaside and Marina** more broadly, for bay views in some neighborhoods and more space for the money.
- **Carmel Valley,** for sunnier weather and larger lots.
- **Salinas, the Highway 68 corridor, and North County,** for the shortest commute north and, on average, more home for the money. That doesn't always mean cheap, though. Salinas proper is where you'll find most of the homes under $1 million. Along Highway 68, in areas like Toro Park, San Benancio, and Corral de Tierra, and in more rural parts of North County like Prunedale, many homes sell for $1 million or more, and some areas come with higher fire insurance costs or a FAIR Plan policy.

The [Buyer's Guide chapter on the areas](/buyers-guide/areas-at-a-glance) has more on each one, and the history posts on the [Neighborhoods page](/neighborhoods) give a feel for each town's character.

## The weather is different, too

The coast can be foggy and cool, especially in summer, while inland areas like Carmel Valley and Salinas get more sun. Even towns a few miles apart can feel different. Marina tends to be foggier than Seaside, and Pacific Grove tends to be foggier than Monterey. If sunshine matters to you, spend time in a neighborhood on a few different days before you buy.

## If you're selling a Bay Area home

Many buyers moving here are also selling a home in the Bay Area. Selling and buying at the same time takes some planning, especially around timing and financing. The [Seller's Guide chapter on selling and buying at the same time](/sellers-guide/selling-and-buying) walks through the options.

If you're 55 or older, Proposition 19 may let you take your current property tax base with you to a new home anywhere in California, which can make a big difference on a long-held Bay Area home. See the [Seller's Guide chapter on what you'll walk away with](/sellers-guide/what-you-walk-away-with) for the basics, and check with the county assessor before you buy.

## Where to focus your own research

- **Before you start looking:** Decide how often you'll need to commute, and test the drive.
- **When you narrow it down:** Spend time in each area, including on a foggy summer morning.
- **For any home you're serious about:** Check the local rules, insurance, and the age of the major systems.
