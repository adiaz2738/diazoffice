If you tried to mail something to Carmel-by-the-Sea before this year, you couldn't put a street number on the envelope. There wasn't one. You'd write something like "Monte Verde 3 SE of Ocean" and hope the post office could work it out, because there was no home delivery either. Everyone in town, all 3,200 or so of them, walked or drove to the post office and picked their mail up out of a box.

That changed this year. As of September 2026, Carmel has real street numbers. City Hall is 662 Monte Verde St. It's on Google Maps now. Plug an address into your GPS and it'll actually take you there.

I've been digging into how this happened, because the story is a lot weirder and a lot longer than "town finally gets addresses." Carmel fought this for over a hundred years. At one point they threatened to secede from California over it. That part's for the next post. This one's about what actually happened in 2026, and why now.

## This isn't about mail delivery

First thing to clear up: nobody's getting mail dropped at their door in Carmel. That was the fear a lot of residents had going into this, that addresses meant mailboxes on every corner and the end of the daily post office run. The city was clear about it the whole way through. Residents keep their PO boxes. Mail still goes to the post office. The addresses exist so that 911 can find you, so the fire code actually gets followed, and so you can order something online without your package sitting on a stranger's porch for two weeks.

That fire code piece matters more than people realize. California requires buildings to have visible address numbers. Carmel just never enforced it on itself. The former fire chief told the city council flat out that the provision had never been enforced in town.

## The real driver was public safety

Carmel has an aging population, median age somewhere around 65 to 69 depending on which year you're reading. No streetlights on residential streets. No sidewalks in a lot of places. People walk around with flashlights at night. Local police could usually respond fast because dispatch knew how to decode the old system, but fire and ambulance calls had to go through county dispatch, which meant explaining where you live twice, sometimes to someone who'd never worked a Carmel call before. A former police chief said it about as well as anyone could: if you're having a heart attack, that's not really the moment you want someone trying to piece together where you actually live.

## How the vote actually went

Getting here took years of votes that kept stalling out. The council tried to move forward in 2024, backed off, tried to punt the whole decision to a public ballot vote in November 2025 instead of just deciding it themselves. Then a new mayor and two new council members got elected in November 2024, and the thing flipped. By October 2025 the council voted 5-0 to move forward. Two months later they passed the ordinance, also 5-0. Even people who'd fought this publicly for years started saying it was time.

The rollout itself turned into its own small saga. The county and USPS had to update their systems. Utilities had to catch up. Google Maps started showing the new numbers in July, before the city had technically finished the process, which the Carmel Pine Cone put right on their cover: addresses showing up before the official green light. The city didn't formally call it done until September.

## Where it landed

Getting numbers didn't turn Carmel into just another town, if that's what people were worried about. Residents fought to keep the custom of naming their houses instead of just numbering them, and the city let them keep doing both. The post office is still there, still the social hub, still no home delivery. What changed is that if something goes wrong, an ambulance can actually find you without decoding a riddle first.

It's a small thing that took over a hundred years and a lot of city councils to actually happen.
