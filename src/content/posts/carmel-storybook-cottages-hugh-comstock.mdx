Carmel's fairy tale cottages, also called storybook cottages, are probably the most famous kind of architecture in town. Steep, uneven roofs. Crooked chimneys. Little arched doors and windows with diamond-shaped panes. People walk the streets looking for them, and once you start spotting them, it's hard to stop.

Like every design trend, this one started somewhere. And it started with a man who had never built a house.

## Hugh Comstock and the doll maker

Hugh Comstock wasn't a builder or a designer, and he had no architectural training when he came to Carmel-by-the-Sea in 1924. He came to visit his sister, Catherine, and her husband, who was part of the Carmel Art Association. While he was here, he met Mayotta Browne. They married that April.

Mayotta was a doll maker. She made Otsy-Totsy dolls, small handmade cloth dolls that sold to stores and collectors, and business was good. So good that the dolls took over the house. She needed somewhere to show them to buyers, and she asked Hugh to build her a little "fairy house in the woods."

## Hansel and Gretel

Hugh built it on Torres Street, between Fifth and Sixth Avenues. It was tiny, about 244 square feet, and he and Mayotta did much of the work themselves. He drew inspiration from the illustrations of English artist Arthur Rackham, whose drawings filled the fairy tale books of the time.

Because Hugh wasn't a trained builder, he didn't build like one. He skipped the level and the plumb line on purpose, so the walls and roof lines lean and curve. The trim around the doors and windows was whittled by hand. The walls were finished with a mix of plaster and pine needles, smoothed by hand. A stonemason built the chimney out of Carmel stone in a deliberately uneven pattern.

They called it the Doll House at first. Today it's known as Hansel. According to the walking tour records, the building materials cost about $100.

The dolls filled Hansel too, so in 1925, Hugh built a second cottage on the same lot, this one for about $400. That one became Gretel.

Those were some good prices back then, weren't they?

## A town-wide sensation

It didn't take long for people to notice. Carmel was already an arts colony, full of painters, writers, and poets, and the cottages fit right in. Neighbors started asking Hugh to build storybook homes for them, too.

Over the next several years, he built cottage after cottage around town, with steep gables, half-timbering, Carmel stone chimneys, and diamond-paned windows. One of them, a cottage called Our House, was built in 1928 for $1,900. About 21 of his cottages are still standing in the Carmel area today.

## The Tuck Box, the residence, and the studio

A few other Comstock buildings are worth looking for:

- **The Tuck Box,** on Dolores Street between Ocean and Seventh Avenues, built in 1926. It started as a shop called the Hobby House and later became a tea room. When it opened, the *Carmel Pine Cone* said it was "built of dreams."
- **The Comstock residence,** on Sixth Avenue near Torres, built in 1925. Hugh and Mayotta lived there, and it doubled as his office for a couple of years.
- **The Studio,** on the corner of Santa Fe Street and Sixth Avenue. By 1927, his business had outgrown the office at home, so he built a studio on the lot next door and added to it over the next decade.

You'll also hear the Cottage of Sweets on Ocean Avenue mentioned as one of Comstock's, and I've said it myself. It looks the part, but it was actually built in 1922 by Lee Gottfried as a weavers' studio, before Comstock arrived, and it was later moved to its current spot. It's a good example of how many builders in Carmel picked up the same storybook look.

## After the fairy tales

By around 1929, Comstock moved on from the fairy tale style. During the Depression, he started making adobe bricks and developed his own way of building with them, which he called Post-Adobe. He wrote a book about it in 1948, and his method was used at Carmel High School.

He also helped shape the town in other ways. He served on the city's planning commission and on the school board, and he helped push for some of the rules that keep Carmel feeling like a village in the forest, like no sidewalks in the residential areas and no home mail delivery. Carmel's long fight against [street addresses](/blog/carmel-by-the-sea-history) came from that same spirit.

Hugh Comstock died in 1950. In 2024, the Carmel City Council passed a proclamation recognizing him and his work.

## If you're buying a storybook cottage

These cottages are a big part of what makes Carmel feel like Carmel, and owning one comes with a few things to know:

- **They're small.** Many were built as cottages in the true sense of the word, on small lots. Hansel is about 244 square feet. Expect small rooms, low ceilings in places, and not much storage.
- **Historic review.** Many of these homes are on Carmel's Historic Resources Inventory. Changes to listed homes go through the city's Historic Resources Board, so a remodel or addition takes more planning. See the [Buyer's Guide chapter on local rules](/buyers-guide/water-permits-local-rules).
- **Older construction.** A home built in the 1920s with hand-built walls and roofs needs a good inspection. Pay attention to the roof, the foundation, the chimney, and the electrical and plumbing.
- **Insurance.** Older homes can be harder or more expensive to insure, so get quotes early. See the [Buyer's Guide chapter on insurance](/buyers-guide/insurance-fire-flood).

## Carmel today

Today, these fantasy cottages are one of the best-known things about Carmel. The architecture is just as unique as the town's history and the people who made it what it is. Carmel-by-the-Sea was built on tradition and creativity, and you can see it on almost every street.
