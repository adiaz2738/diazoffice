Most people know Carmel as the town that artists and writers settled in. What I didn't know until I started reading about it is that a different group of people was supposed to settle there first, and that the plan was Catholic.

In Kent Seavey's book on Carmel's architecture there's a photo of an old advertisement. It reads "Carmel City, Monterey Co. Cal. Catholic Summer Resort. S. J. Duckworth, Real Estate Agent, Alvarado St., Monterey Cal." Next to it is a portrait of Santiago Duckworth. The caption says Carmel City was meant to become the first Catholic summer colony in California, and that it was based on the success of the Methodist retreat in Pacific Grove.

So to understand Carmel City, you kind of have to start in Pacific Grove.

## The Methodist retreat next door

Pacific Grove started as a religious camp. Methodist leaders talked about a seaside retreat on the West Coast at an annual conference in San Francisco in the early 1870s. The Pacific Grove Retreat Association was formed in June 1875, and by July they had signed a deal with landowner David Jacks for 100 acres. The first camp meeting ran August 8 through 29 of that year.

It grew from there. The Pacific Improvement Company, which was the land arm of the Southern Pacific Railroad, bought the rest of the land in 1880. In 1883 the two groups signed a contract that split the work. The company handled the money, and the retreat association handled what the contract called "the moral and prudential management" of the town. Pacific Grove became its own city in 1889, and the railroad arrived the same year.

That's the model the Duckworth brothers were looking at.

## Carmel City, 1888

Santiago and Belisario Duckworth went into the real estate business in Monterey in 1887. Santiago was born in Monterey in 1865, so he was only about 23 when Carmel City went on sale. He also sold insurance, and in 1893 he served in the State Assembly. According to a 1967 article from the Monterey History and Art Association, the goal was a Catholic retreat in Carmel. The title of that article is "A Catholic Carmel To Rival Methodist Pacific Grove," which tells you pretty much how the author saw it.

There's some backdrop here too. Father Angel Casanova had been restoring the mission in the early 1880s. Serra's grave was opened in July 1882, and the church was rededicated in August 1884. I can't say that directly led to Carmel City, and I haven't seen a source that says it did, but the timing is close enough that it's worth knowing about.

Here's how the timeline goes, based on that 1967 article:

- **February 18, 1888:** the Duckworths bond 324 acres of Honore Escolle's land.
- **May 1, 1888:** the survey map is filed. The survey was done by W. C. Little and his apprentice, Davenport Bromfield.
- **Around May 15, 1888:** about 700 lots in the first 94 acres go on sale.
- **End of 1888:** an 18-room hotel is built.
- **Mid-1889:** stagecoach service is arranged for prospective buyers.
- **1890:** Santiago Duckworth tells the *Monterey Cypress* that a seminary will be built along Valley Way for $10,000.

That seminary is a good detail. The idea was that prominent Catholics would pay for it, and then it would be handed over to the Church for education. It never happened.

## What went wrong

Lots sold slowly. By 1891 the Duckworths, who were acting as agents for Escolle's land, couldn't carry the debt anymore. A new company, run by Abbie Jane Hunter, took over in early 1892, and it dropped the religious angle completely. Several hundred lots did sell between 1892 and 1902, but in 1903 Hunter sold to San Francisco attorney Frank Powers.

As for why it didn't work, the sources don't give the same reason.

The City of Carmel's own historic context statement says the Duckworths' plans fell apart when "the boom of the 1880s quickly turned into the depression of the 1890s." The 1967 article points somewhere else. It says the tract was too isolated and transportation was too erratic.

Those two could both be true at the same time. And there's another detail in the city's report that I keep thinking about: the railroad extension to Carmel never happened. Pacific Grove had a railroad-owned partner and a train by 1889. Carmel had a stagecoach. I haven't found a source that says this is the reason one worked and the other didn't, so I'm not going to claim that. It's just a pretty big difference between the two places.

## What Carmel turned into instead

The 1967 article ends by saying Carmel became "a town somewhat less religious than the Duckworth brothers had imagined." That might be the understatement of the whole history of the town.

The next chapter of the story is the one people know. Frank Powers and James Devendorf pitched Carmel to artists and writers. Seavey's book says Powers' wife, Jane Gallatin Powers, was a European-trained painter who turned an old log barn above Carmel Beach into a painting studio and salon, where she hosted friends from San Francisco's bohemian art community. He credits her as a strong influence in promoting Carmel as an artists' colony.

So the town that was supposed to be a Catholic summer camp ended up with a salon in a log barn instead.

## Inconsistencies in the sources

The city's historic context statement says Santiago Duckworth bought the 324 acres from Escolle. Seavey's caption and the 1967 article both describe a bonded agreement instead, and the 1967 article calls the Duckworths agents for Escolle's land. Two out of three say bonded agreement, so that's the version in this post.

The date Hunter's company took over also changes depending on where you look. The 1967 article says early 1892, which is what I used above. A local wiki page puts a land purchase by Hunter in August 1891, and I couldn't tell whether those are the same event.

That same wiki page has a few extra details, like who named the early Paradise Park neighborhood. It doesn't say where any of that came from, so I left it out.

The 1967 article and the city's historic context statement are both free to read online, and the links are in the sources list. Seavey's book is the one to borrow from the library.
