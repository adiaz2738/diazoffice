*Last checked: September 2026. Every area here has a wide range of homes. Which one fits you depends on the lifestyle you want, your budget, your commute, and what you plan to do with the house.*

Monterey County is big. It runs from the Pajaro River at the Santa Cruz County line down past King City, and from the coast to the hills on the east side of the Salinas Valley. Most people who buy here end up choosing between a handful of areas, and each one comes with its own mix of homes, prices, and local rules.

This chapter gives you a quick lay of the land. For a map and more detail on individual neighborhoods, see the [Neighborhoods page](/neighborhoods).

## Start with the lifestyle you want

One of the best things about living here is that you can find almost any lifestyle within about an hour's drive:

- **Rural, with room to spread out:** Prunedale, Corral de Tierra, and Carmel Valley.
- **By the ocean:** Pacific Grove and parts of Monterey.
- **A small village feel:** Carmel-by-the-Sea.
- **On or near a golf course:** Pebble Beach, or communities like Seaside Highlands and The Enclave in Seaside.
- **Farm-town living with newer homes:** the Salinas Valley towns.

What you won't really find is big-city living. There are great restaurants and a lively downtown or two, but nightlife isn't a big part of local culture.

Most areas also have a wider range of homes than people expect. In Monterey, Pacific Grove, and Carmel, you can find anything from a small cottage close to the neighbors to a large home with more space around it. Pebble Beach leans more spread out, with bigger lots throughout.

So before you pick a town, it helps to think about how you want to live day to day. The area usually follows from that.

## The Monterey Peninsula

The Peninsula is what most people picture when they think of this area: the coast, the cypress and pine forests, and the older homes. It's also where the water rules from [Chapter 6](/buyers-guide/water-permits-local-rules) apply most, since most of it is served by California American Water and falls inside the Monterey Peninsula Water Management District.

**Monterey.** The county's historic center, and California's capital under Spanish and Mexican rule. Housing ranges from older homes and small cottages near downtown and New Monterey to newer neighborhoods on the hills. The city requires its own inspection report before a sale, and short-term rentals under 30 days aren't allowed.

**Pacific Grove.** Started in 1875 as a Methodist retreat, and you can still see it in the small Victorian cottages near the water. Many older homes, a long list of historic properties, and a city that reviews changes to them closely. Tree rules and a pre-sale city report apply here too.

**Carmel-by-the-Sea.** A small village of storybook cottages under a heavy tree canopy. Homes tend to be small lots with older houses, and the city has some of the strictest design, tree, and historic review in the county. Short-term rentals aren't allowed in the single-family zone.

**Pebble Beach.** An unincorporated, gated community in the Del Monte Forest, home to 17-Mile Drive and the golf courses. Mostly larger single-family homes on bigger lots. Because it's unincorporated, the county handles permits, and tree removal also involves the Pebble Beach Company.

**Seaside and Sand City.** Seaside has a mix of older post-war homes, newer developments, and neighborhoods near the former Fort Ord, and it's often one of the more affordable places to buy on the Peninsula. Sand City is tiny, with more commercial and industrial land than homes.

**Del Rey Oaks.** A small, mostly residential city tucked between Monterey and Seaside, with a quieter, suburban layout.

**Marina.** The northern end of the bay, and technically just outside the Peninsula's water district, with its own water provider. Marina has older neighborhoods plus newer communities on former Fort Ord land. Fort Ord closed as an Army base in 1994, and much of it has since become CSU Monterey Bay, the Fort Ord National Monument, and new housing.

## Carmel Valley, Carmel Highlands, and Big Sur

**Carmel Valley.** Runs inland along the Carmel River. It's generally sunnier and warmer than the coast, with homes ranging from neighborhoods near the mouth of the valley to larger properties and ranches farther in. It's unincorporated, so the county handles permits. Some properties have wells or septic systems, parts of the valley are in higher fire hazard zones, and homes near the river can be in a flood zone. Commercial short-term rentals aren't allowed in its residential zones.

**Carmel Highlands and Big Sur.** Dramatic coastline with fewer homes and more rules. Expect coastal permits, fire and access considerations, and very limited inventory. Big Sur in particular is remote, and Highway 1 closures from landslides can cut off parts of the coast for long stretches.

## The Highway 68 corridor

Between Monterey and Salinas, Highway 68 runs past unincorporated areas like Monterey Ranch, Laguna Seca, Corral de Tierra, and Toro Park. It's popular with people who want more space and a location between the Peninsula and Salinas. Homes are often larger, on bigger lots, and some use wells or septic systems. It's worth checking water source, fire zone, and commute for any specific property.

## Salinas and the Salinas Valley

**Salinas.** The county seat and its largest city, built on agriculture, which earned the valley the nickname "The Salad Bowl of the World." Salinas has the widest range of homes in the county, from older neighborhoods near downtown to newer subdivisions on the north and east sides. It's generally more affordable than the Peninsula, and it's closer to Highway 101 for commuting north. Salinas has its own rental rules right now, as covered in Chapter 6, and it doesn't have a short-term rental permit program.

**The Salinas Valley.** South along Highway 101 are Gonzales, Soledad, Greenfield, and King City, surrounded by farmland and vineyards. These towns are generally the most affordable in the county, with newer subdivisions alongside older homes. They're farther from the coast, so commutes to the Peninsula are longer.

## North County

North of Salinas and Marina are unincorporated communities like Castroville, Prunedale, Moss Landing, Aromas, and Pajaro. Housing ranges from small-town neighborhoods to rural properties with land, and many rural homes use wells and septic systems. Parts of North County, especially near the Pajaro River, have a history of flooding, as covered in [Chapter 7](/buyers-guide/insurance-fire-flood). North County is also the closest part of the county to Santa Cruz and the Bay Area.

## How to narrow it down

A few questions usually do most of the work:

- **What's your budget?** Prices vary a lot across the county. Your agent can show you recent sales in the areas you're considering.
- **Where do you need to get to?** Traffic on Highways 1, 68, and 101 can make short distances feel long. Try the drive at the time you'd actually be making it.
- **What do you want to do with the house?** Adding a bathroom, building an ADU, or renting it out depends heavily on the city and water district, as covered in Chapter 6.
- **How do you want to live?** Near the water, in the country, in a small village, or on a golf course. Coastal fog and forest, or a sunnier valley.

Once you've narrowed it to an area or two, spend time there. Walk the neighborhood, drive it at different times of day, and see how it feels.
